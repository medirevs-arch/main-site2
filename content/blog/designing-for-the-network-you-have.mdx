There is a version of digital health that works beautifully in a demo and fails
in a corridor. The demo has full signal. The corridor has one bar, a shared
handset, and a queue.

Building for the second case changes the software, not just its settings.

## Connectivity is a state, not a switch

Treating the network as either on or off gives you software that behaves badly
in the state most people are actually in, which is neither. The
connection is present but slow. It drops mid-request. It returns while a form is
half-filled.

Designing for that means a few unglamorous commitments:

- **Writes are local first.** The user's action completes on their device. Sync
  is a background concern, not something they wait for.
- **Conflict has a defined answer.** Two clinicians editing the same record on
  two devices is a normal event, not an exception to handle later.
- **State is visible.** People trust a system that tells them plainly what has
  synced and what has not. They stop trusting one that pretends everything is
  fine.

## The phone is the computer

Across much of the continent, the primary computing device in a clinical setting
is a mid range Android phone, often someone's personal one. That is not a
limitation to design around. It is the platform.

It sets real budgets. Pages have to be small, because data costs money the user
is paying personally. Interfaces have to work one-handed, because the other hand
is doing something. Text has to stay legible on a screen in daylight.

It also sets an honest test for anything we build: if it is unpleasant on a
three-year-old handset on a weak connection, it is not finished.

## What this rules out

This approach rules some things out, and it is worth being direct about them.

Heavy client-side applications that assume a fast download are out. Interfaces
that fetch on every keystroke are out. Media-heavy screens that look impressive
in a screenshot and cost a user real money to load are out.

What remains is a narrower, more disciplined kind of software. We think that
discipline is the product.
